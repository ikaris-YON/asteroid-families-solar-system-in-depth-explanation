Small bodies of the Solar System

Asteroid families, and every place small worlds live

An asteroid family is the debris of one shattered or cratered parent body, still travelling on similar orbits millions to billions of years later. This guide walks outward from the Sun: near-Earth asteroids, the main belt and its families, the Hildas and Trojans, the Kuiper belt and the Oort cloud. Every figure on the page can be played with.

01 · The reservoirs

Where small bodies live

Small bodies are not spread evenly. They sit in a handful of reservoirs, each shaped by the gravity of the giant planets. Some regions are stable for the age of the Solar System; others are leaky and constantly drain into new ones. Use the three scales below. In the inner view, switch to rotating with Jupiter and watch the Hildas fold into a triangle and the Trojans gather at two points 60° ahead of and behind the planet.

Inner Solar System

Near-Earth asteroids have perihelion q < 1.3 AU. Their orbits last only a few million years before they hit the Sun, a planet, or are thrown out, so the population is steadily resupplied from the main belt.

Hungarias (1.78–2.0 AU) sit inside the belt at high inclination (16–34°), protected from Mars by tilt. The main belt (2.1–3.3 AU) holds most asteroids, cut into inner, middle and outer zones by Jupiter resonances. Cybeles (3.3–3.7 AU) lie just outside the 2:1 resonance.

Hildas (≈3.97 AU) make exactly 3 orbits for every 2 of Jupiter's. Jupiter Trojans share Jupiter's orbit around the L4 and L5 Lagrange points; L4 (leading) holds noticeably more of them than L5.

Giant planets to the Kuiper belt

Centaurs wander between Jupiter and Neptune on orbits that last only a few million years. They are Kuiper belt objects on their way to becoming Jupiter-family comets.

Plutinos (39.4 AU) orbit twice for every three Neptune orbits, the same 3:2 lock Pluto has. In Neptune's rotating frame their perihelia always avoid Neptune. Twotinos sit in the 2:1 at 47.8 AU.

Classical Kuiper belt objects (≈42–48 AU) come in two kinds: a dynamically cold, low-inclination, red, binary-rich group thought to have formed in place (Arrokoth is one), and a hot group with larger inclinations. The scattered disk has perihelia near Neptune and long, eccentric orbits.

Out to the Oort cloud

Radius here is logarithmic: every ring is ten times farther than the one inside it. The planets crowd into the centre. Hover to read distances.

The inner (Hills) cloud spans roughly 2,000–20,000 AU and the outer Oort cloud about 20,000 to beyond 100,000 AU, where the Sun's grip weakens against the Galaxy's tide. No Oort cloud object has been seen in place: we infer it from long-period comets falling inward on nearly parabolic orbits.

Detached objects such as Sedna (perihelion ≈76 AU) never come close to Neptune and point to something that shaped the outer Solar System long ago, perhaps the Sun's birth cluster.

02 · Jupiter's fingerprints

Why the belt has gaps and walls

In 1866 Daniel Kirkwood noticed that almost no asteroids have periods that are simple fractions of Jupiter's 11.86 years. These mean-motion resonances (MMRs) are written as p:q: an asteroid in the 3:1 resonance orbits three times for each Jupiter orbit. Because the pattern repeats, Jupiter tugs the asteroid at the same points of its orbit again and again, and the small kicks add up instead of cancelling.

The resonance location follows from Kepler's third law: a = aJ (q/p)2/3, with aJ = 5.20 AU. In strong resonances such as 3:1, 5:2 and 2:1 the motion is chaotic and the eccentricity is pumped until the orbit crosses Mars or Earth. That removes asteroids and is the main delivery route for near-Earth asteroids and meteorites.

There is a second kind: secular resonances, where the slow precession of an asteroid's orbit matches a planet's precession. The ν6 resonance (perihelion precession matching Saturn's, g = g6) forms the inner edge of the main belt near 2.1 AU and curves to higher inclinations further out. It is the single biggest supplier of near-Earth asteroids.

03 · The core idea

What makes an asteroid family

In 1918 the Japanese astronomer Kiyotsugu Hirayama found that some asteroids share almost identical orbits once you strip away the wobbles caused by the planets. He named the first groups after their largest members: Koronis, Eos and Themis. He proposed they were fragments of broken bodies. A century of data has confirmed it.

How families form

Catastrophic disruption. A projectile hits hard enough to shatter the parent, and the largest remnant keeps less than half its mass. Koronis, Eos and Themis are of this kind. The fragments leave at speeds of tens to a few hundred metres per second, tiny compared with orbital speeds near 18 km/s, so they stay on neighbouring orbits.

Cratering. A big crater blasts out fragments while the parent survives almost whole. Vesta's family came mostly from the Rheasilvia basin, about 1 billion years ago. Its fragments are basaltic (V-type), which is why we can link the HED meteorites on Earth to Vesta.

Rotational fission and pairs. Sunlight (the YORP effect) can spin a small asteroid up until it splits. This makes asteroid pairs and tiny clusters only thousands to a few million years old.

Proper elements: the fingerprint

The everyday orbit you look up, the osculating elements, changes from week to week as the planets pull on it. Family members' osculating orbits drift apart in phase and quickly look unrelated. Proper elements (ap, ep, sin ip) average those oscillations out. They stay nearly constant for hundreds of millions of years, so they preserve the family's original clustering. Outer belt, osculating vs proper elements

Same asteroids in both views. The osculating eccentricity of each body is its proper eccentricity plus a forced part from Jupiter, combined at a random phase, so the clumps smear out. Switch to proper elements and Koronis, Eos, Themis, Hygiea and Veritas reappear.

Families at a glance

The belt, zone by zone

Inner belt (2.1–2.5 AU), between ν6 and the 3:1. Home of Vesta, Flora, Nysa–Polana, Massalia, Baptistina and Erigone. It is the nearest reservoir to Earth, so its families dominate meteorite falls. The dark Polana and Eulalia families are the likely sources of Bennu and Ryugu, the asteroids sampled by OSIRIS-REx and Hayabusa2.

Middle belt (2.5–2.82 AU), between 3:1 and 5:2. Eunomia, Maria, Gefion, Adeona, Juno, Dora and the steeply tilted Pallas family.

"Pristine zone" (2.82–2.96 AU), between 5:2 and 7:3. Few big resonances cross it, so fewer bodies drift in from outside. Koronis lives here, with the 5.8-million-year-old Karin cluster nested inside it.

Outer belt (2.96–3.28 AU), out to the 2:1. Large, mostly dark families: Eos, Themis, Hygiea, Ursula, Veritas and Alauda. Several Themis members show comet-like activity (main-belt comets), and ice has been detected on (24) Themis itself.

A notable absence: Ceres, the largest body in the belt, has no detectable family. Its icy crust may have absorbed or masked fragments, or they may be too dark and small to see yet.

05 · Method

How families are found: hierarchical clustering

The standard tool is the Hierarchical Clustering Method (HCM; Zappalà et al. 1990). It measures the "distance" between two asteroids in proper elements as the velocity kick that would move one orbit into the other:

d = n a √( 5/4 (δa/a)² + 2 (δe)² + 2 (δ sin i)² ) [m/s]

Here n a is the orbital speed. Two asteroids are linked if d is below a cut-off velocity vc, and a family is any chain of linked bodies above a minimum size. Choosing vc is the hard part. Too small and real families break into pieces. Too large and families chain together through the background into one blob. Drag the slider and watch both failures happen.

The size distribution and the V-shape in the next section help decide whether a cluster is a real family. Newer methods use machine learning and spectral data together with orbits.

06 · Time

How families spread, and how we date them

A spinning asteroid absorbs sunlight and re-emits it as heat in a slightly different direction. That tiny recoil, the Yarkovsky effect, slowly changes the semimajor axis. Prograde spinners drift outward, retrograde spinners drift inward, and small bodies drift faster because the force scales with area while mass scales with volume. A related torque, YORP, changes the spin and tends to push spin axes toward the extremes, which makes the drift steadier.

Plot each member's semimajor axis against 1/D and a family takes the shape of a V. Its opening angle grows with age. That makes the V a clock: older families have wider Vs. Move the slider.

Fragments that reach a resonance wall are removed and start the journey toward near-Earth space (section 07). Families near a resonance are therefore steady sources of NEAs and meteorites, and the smallest members leave first.

The dashed V shows the maximum drift for spin axes perpendicular to the orbit. Fitting that envelope to a real family gives its age.

Other clocks

Backward integration. For families only a few million years old, the slowly precessing orbits of members can be run backward until they line up. Karin's members converge 5.75 million years ago (Nesvorný et al. 2002). Veritas converges about 8.3 million years ago and matches a spike of extraterrestrial dust (helium-3) in late Miocene ocean sediments. The tiny Datura cluster is only about 450,000 years old.

Space weathering. Solar wind and micrometeorites slowly redden and darken S-type surfaces, so the colours of young families are fresher.

Crater counts on the parent (Vesta's Rheasilvia basin, seen by NASA's Dawn) give an independent date.

Families and meteorites. Studies published in Nature in 2024 traced most ordinary chondrite meteorites to a few young families: the Massalia family for L chondrites, and the Karin and Koronis families for H chondrites. Young families still hold many small fragments, which is why they dominate the meteorite supply.
07 · Near-Earth space

Near-Earth asteroids: the belt's overflow

A near-Earth asteroid (NEA) is one with perihelion distance q < 1.3 AU. NEAs are not a family; they are a transient population fed mainly by the main belt. A NEA typically survives a few million years, ten at most, before it falls into the Sun, hits a planet, or is flung out by Jupiter. To keep their numbers steady, the main belt has to keep resupplying them through resonances.

They are divided by orbit into four classes, named after a prototype of each (with perihelion q = a(1 − e) and aphelion Q = a(1 + e)):

a < 1 AU · Q < 0.983 AU

Atiras

Orbits entirely inside Earth's. Hard to find because they are always in the twilight sky. The rarer Vatiras stay inside Venus's orbit too (Q < 0.718 AU); 'Ayló'chaxnim (2020 AV2) was the first found.

a < 1 AU · Q > 0.983 AU

Atens

Earth-crossing, but with periods under one year, so they spend most of their time inside Earth's orbit. Apophis is an Aten.

a > 1 AU · q < 1.017 AU

Apollos

Earth-crossing with periods over a year. The largest class, and the home of most potentially hazardous asteroids. Bennu, Ryugu, Itokawa, Didymos and Phaethon are Apollos.

1.017 < q < 1.3 AU

Amors

Approach Earth's orbit from outside without crossing it today. Many cross Mars's orbit. Eros, the first NEA found (1898), is an Amor.

The Tisserand parameter TJ = aJ/a + 2 cos i √((a/aJ)(1 − e²)) is nearly conserved during encounters with Jupiter. TJ > 3 is typical of asteroids, 2 < TJ < 3 of Jupiter-family comets, and TJ < 2 of long-period and Halley-type comets.

Where NEAs come from

Granvik et al. (2018) fitted a model of the NEA population to survey data and traced each NEA back to one of seven escape routes: the ν6, 3:1, 5:2 and 2:1 resonances, the Hungaria and Phocaea regions, and Jupiter-family comets. The share from each route depends strongly on size. Large NEAs come from many routes, while the smallest come almost entirely from the nearby inner-belt routes (ν6, 3:1 and Hungaria).

Planetary defence

A potentially hazardous asteroid (PHA) has a minimum orbit intersection distance with Earth under 0.05 AU and H < 22 (roughly larger than 140 m). NASA's DART spacecraft hit Dimorphos, the moon of Didymos, in September 2022 and shortened its orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes. ESA's Hera is due to begin close operations at Didymos in December 2026 to measure the result in detail. Apophis will pass Earth at about 32,000 km on 13 April 2029, inside the ring of geostationary satellites, and NASA's OSIRIS-APEX will follow it. NASA's NEO Surveyor infrared telescope is being built to find dark NEAs that ground surveys miss.

08 · Past the 2:1 resonance

Beyond the main belt

Families are not limited to the main belt. Wherever bodies are numerous enough to collide, they can form, and resonances outside the belt hold their own populations. Use the buttons to jump to each group on the map.

≈1.5 AU · Mars L4/L5

Mars Trojans

A handful of bodies share Mars's orbit. Most at L5 cluster around (5261) Eureka and look like a tiny family, likely formed by YORP spin-off rather than a collision. Several are olivine-rich.

1.78–2.0 AU · i 16–34°

Hungarias

Dominated by the Hungaria family of bright enstatite (E-type) asteroids. Close to Mars, they leak steadily into near-Earth space and are a major source of small NEAs.

3.3–3.7 AU

Cybeles

A sparse group beyond the 2:1, bounded by the 5:3 and 7:4 resonances. Mostly primitive dark types; (87) Sylvia with its two moons is the best known.

3.97 AU · 3:2 with Jupiter

Hildas

Locked in a 3:2 resonance, they never get close to Jupiter: in Jupiter's frame they trace a triangle with corners at L3, L4 and L5. Two collisional families are known here, Hilda and Schubart. Dark D- and P-types.

5.2 AU · Jupiter L4/L5

Jupiter Trojans

Over ten thousand known, librating around Jupiter's L4 and L5 points. They likely were captured from the outer disk when the giant planets migrated. Several families exist, the best known being Eurybates (L4) and Ennomos (L5). NASA's Lucy spacecraft flies past Eurybates and its moon Queta in August 2027.

5–30 AU · short-lived

Centaurs

Escapees from the Kuiper belt and scattered disk on unstable orbits among the giant planets, with lifetimes of a few million years. Some show comet activity (Chiron); Chariklo has rings. Many end as Jupiter-family comets.

≈30 AU · Neptune L4/L5

Neptune Trojans

Dozens known, and possibly more numerous than Jupiter's Trojans. Many have high inclinations, a clue that they were captured during Neptune's migration.

30–50 AU · Kuiper belt

Kuiper belt

Resonant objects (plutinos at 3:2, twotinos at 2:1), a cold classical belt formed in place, a hot classical belt, and the scattered disk. Pluto, Eris, Makemake, Haumea and Arrokoth live here.

a ≈ 43 AU · i ≈ 28°

The Haumea family

The only confirmed collisional family in the Kuiper belt. About ten bodies share Haumea's orbit and its nearly pure water-ice surface. Haumea spins in under four hours and has a ring and two moons, all consistent with a giant impact long ago. The family's velocity spread is unexpectedly small, which still puzzles modellers.

Planetary moons

Irregular satellite families

The small, distant, often retrograde moons of the giant planets fall into tight groups with similar orbits and colours: Jupiter's Himalia, Ananke and Carme groups, and Saturn's Inuit, Gallic and Norse groups. Each group is probably the debris of one captured moon broken by a collision.

09 · The far edge

The Oort cloud, comets and interstellar visitors

In 1950 Jan Oort noticed that many long-period comets arrive with semimajor axes of tens of thousands of AU, the "Oort spike". He proposed a vast spherical swarm of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System. These bodies were not born there. They formed among the giant planets and were scattered outward, and passing stars and the Galaxy's tide then lifted their perihelia so they never returned. The same forces now occasionally nudge one back inward as a new long-period comet.

The cloud is thought to contain hundreds of billions to a trillion kilometre-sized comets, yet with a total mass of perhaps a few Earth masses. It may reach a third of the way to Proxima Centauri. Some of its members may have been captured from other stars in the Sun's birth cluster.

Do comets form families?

Not in the collisional sense, because comets rarely collide. But they do fragment. The Kreutz sungrazers are hundreds of pieces of one great comet that broke up near the Sun, and meteor showers trace the debris trails of single comets and asteroids, such as the Geminids from (3200) Phaethon. Jupiter-family comets share Jupiter-controlled orbits (2 < TJ < 3) but are a dynamical class, not siblings.

Interstellar objects

Three objects from other star systems have been seen passing through: 1I/ʻOumuamua (2017), the comet 2I/Borisov (2019), and the comet 3I/ATLAS (2025). Their hyperbolic orbits (e > 1) show they are unbound to the Sun. If other stars eject their small bodies this freely, the Oort clouds of neighbouring stars may overlap with ours.

10 · Reference

Glossary

Semimajor axis a
Half the long axis of the orbit; sets the period through Kepler's third law, P (years) = a1.5 (AU).
Eccentricity e
How stretched the ellipse is: 0 is a circle, near 1 is very elongated.
Inclination i
Tilt of the orbit to the ecliptic. Family plots use sin i.
q and Q
Perihelion a(1 − e) and aphelion a(1 + e) distances.
Osculating elements
The instantaneous Keplerian orbit; it changes as planets perturb the body.
Proper elements
Elements with periodic perturbations averaged out; quasi-constant over long times, used to find families.
Mean-motion resonance
Orbital periods in a simple ratio p:q with a planet's, so perturbations repeat and add up.
Secular resonance
Precession rate of an asteroid's perihelion (g) or node (s) matching a planet's; ν6 is g = g6, ν16 is s = s6.
Yarkovsky effect
Thermal recoil that slowly changes the semimajor axis of small bodies.
YORP effect
Thermal torque that changes spin rate and spin-axis direction; can spin bodies up until they split.
HCM
Hierarchical Clustering Method; links asteroids closer than a cut-off velocity in proper elements.
Taxonomic class
Spectral type: S (stony, silicate), C (carbonaceous, dark), V (basaltic, Vesta-like), X (mixed, includes metal-rich M and enstatite E), K, B, D/P (very dark, red, outer Solar System).
Tisserand parameter TJ
A near-invariant during Jupiter encounters used to separate asteroid-like from comet-like orbits.
PHA
Potentially hazardous asteroid: MOID < 0.05 AU and H < 22.
